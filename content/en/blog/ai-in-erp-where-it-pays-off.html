Hardly any ERP vendor launches a release today without an AI story. SAP promotes its assistant Joule, Microsoft offers Copilot capabilities in Dynamics 365, and other vendors are positioning AI features of their own. For CIOs, CFOs and heads of IT, the real question is therefore not whether AI will arrive in ERP, but where it actually pays off in their own organisation – and where it simply adds complexity.

In projects I tend to see two extremes: AI is either dismissed out of hand, or it turns into a long wish list with no clear objective. This article sets out where AI in ERP makes sense, where it does not, and how to approach it in a structured way. The principles apply to SAP just as much as to Microsoft Dynamics 365, Oracle, Infor, proALPHA, abas, Odoo and other ERP systems.

AI in ERP: what it does well – and what it doesn't

At its core, an ERP system is deterministic. A posting follows fixed rules, a stock level is a number, an approval follows a defined workflow. Generative AI and machine learning, by contrast, work with probabilities. That leads to a simple rule of thumb:

AI pays off where people currently read, interpret and combine unstructured information. It does not pay off where the ERP already applies clear rules reliably.

Its strengths therefore lie mainly at the edges of the ERP: turning documents, emails and know-how into structured data, and pulling together information from several systems.

Where AI in ERP pays off

The use cases below share two features: they involve a lot of manual reading and searching, and a person can check the result.

A note on incoming invoices in Germany

Since 1 January 2025, businesses in Germany must be able to receive domestic B2B e-invoices. Transitional rules apply to issuing them until the end of 2026 or 2027, and from 2028 the requirement covers all domestic businesses (IHK Heilbronn-Franken). Structured formats such as XRechnung or ZUGFeRD can be read without any AI. The value of AI therefore shifts away from recognising fields and towards matching, resolving discrepancies and handling documents that still arrive unstructured. This is not tax advice; please check the details with your tax adviser.

Where AI does not belong in ERP

Just as important is the list of cases where AI is more likely to hurt than help:

  • Deterministic posting logic: tax determination, account determination, valuation rules and closing entries are clearly defined. Properly configured standard logic is more reliable, more transparent and easier to audit.
  • Poor data quality: if master data is duplicated, outdated or inconsistent, AI will not give better answers, just more convincingly worded wrong ones. Clean data comes before AI.
  • Unclear processes: a process nobody can describe cannot be automated sensibly either.
  • Decisions with legal effect and no human control: where decisions significantly affect individuals, the GDPR already limits solely automated decision-making (Article 22 GDPR).

The ground rule: AI posts nothing without approval

To get started, I recommend one clear principle: AI prepares, a person approves. The AI proposes an account assignment, explains a variance or drafts a supplier request. Nothing is posted, ordered or sent until an authorised person has consciously approved it.

This has several advantages:

  • Your existing authorisation and approval concept in the ERP stays valid.
  • AI errors become visible before they have consequences.
  • Auditors can follow a clear chain of responsibility.
  • Users stay in control and build trust in the tool.

Technically, this means the AI should access the ERP through released interfaces rather than writing directly to tables. Check the licensing side too. With SAP, for instance, access by external systems can fall under the document-based "Digital Access" model (E-3 Magazin). There is more on this in the article Optimising SAP licences.

Data protection and the EU AI Act: what already applies

Two frameworks should be considered from day one. What follows is orientation only, not legal advice.

Data protection

As soon as personal data – about employees, supplier contacts or customers – is passed to an AI model, the usual questions apply: legal basis, data processing agreements, storage location, deletion and purpose limitation. Clarify early which data the model needs to see and which it does not.

AI literacy under Article 4 of the AI Act

The AI literacy provision has applied to providers and deployers of AI systems since 2 February 2025 (European Commission). The Digital Omnibus on AI, in force since 27 July 2026, softened Article 4: organisations must take measures to support AI literacy among their staff, but no longer have to guarantee a specific level of competence for each individual (Lewis Silkin). In practice: run training and briefings, and document them. Obligations for high-risk systems under Annex III have been postponed to 2 December 2027.

Typical ERP use cases such as invoice processing or knowledge search are unlikely to be classed as high-risk. AI used in HR, for example in candidate selection, may be a different matter. Have the classification checked case by case.

The approach: small pilots with measurable goals

Rather than launching a large AI programme, I recommend a step-by-step route:

  1. Pick one process where people currently spend noticeable time reading, searching or reconciling.
  2. Measure the baseline: lead time, number of queries, error rate, effort per case – using your own figures.
  3. Agree the target and a stop criterion before the pilot begins.
  4. Test with real but limited data and a small group of users.
  5. Build in an approval step and analyse every difference between the AI's proposal and the human decision.
  6. Decide: roll out, adjust, or deliberately stop.

A pilot you stop is not a failure; it is an inexpensive lesson.

If your ERP is due for modernisation anyway – for example moving from SAP ECC to S/4HANA – it is worth building AI use cases into the target architecture early. For background, see SAP ECC end of maintenance 2027.

Checklist for getting started

  • Is there a specific process with a high manual reading or search workload?
  • Is the required master data clean enough?
  • Is it defined who approves AI proposals?
  • Does access run through released interfaces, and is licensing clarified?
  • Have data protection and data location been addressed?
  • Have the users involved been trained, and is that documented?
  • Are there measurable goals and a stop criterion?

FAQ

Do I need a new ERP system to use AI?

Not necessarily. Many use cases can be built alongside your existing system via interfaces. Vendors' built-in AI features are sometimes tied to newer releases or cloud editions, so check this for your situation.

Can AI post directly in the ERP?

Technically, often yes. To start with, I advise against it: the AI should make proposals, and postings happen after approval by an authorised person.

How much will AI in ERP save us?

There is no credible blanket answer. That is exactly why every pilot should start by measuring the baseline – so you see the effect in your own numbers.
