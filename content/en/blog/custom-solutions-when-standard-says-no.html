"The standard can't do that." Business teams hear this sentence in almost every ERP project. Often it is a fair answer: an ERP standard reflects proven processes, and every deviation adds operating and maintenance effort. Sometimes, though, the discussion stops too early – and a process that genuinely sets the business apart ends up in spreadsheets, email threads and side lists.

So the question is not whether you are allowed to have custom SAP solutions or extensions to another ERP system. It is when they make sense, and how to build them so your core system stays stable and upgradeable. The principles here apply to SAP as much as to Microsoft Dynamics 365, Oracle, Infor, proALPHA, abas or Odoo; SAP is simply one example among several.

"No" usually means "not in the standard". It rarely means "not at all".

Where the ERP standard reaches its limits

In SAP projects, I mostly come across five situations in which the standard on its own is not enough:

  • Industry-specific processes: workflows typical of your sector or business model that neither the core product nor an industry solution covers adequately.
  • Interfaces: connections to machines, portals, partners or public authorities for which no ready-made connector exists.
  • Legacy systems: specialist applications that cannot be retired (yet) and still need to exchange data with the ERP.
  • Carve-outs: data, processes and authorisations have to be separated, often under time pressure and with transitional service agreements between seller and buyer.
  • Consolidation: after acquisitions, several ERP systems run in parallel, and reporting and control have to work across all of them before harmonisation is complete.

In each case, it is worth first checking carefully whether the standard could fit after all – by changing the process rather than the software. Only once the honest answer is no does the question of how arise.

Clean core: extend outside the core

The most important rule for any custom solution is simple: leave the core alone. SAP calls this "clean core". According to SAP, the aim is to keep the core "clean, standard, and upgrade-stable" while still allowing business-specific differentiation (SAP News).

SAP distinguishes two main routes:

  • On-stack extensions inside the ERP, built with ABAP Cloud and using only released interfaces.
  • Side-by-side extensions on SAP Business Technology Platform (BTP), running as separate applications next to the core.

On top of this, SAP uses a level model from A to D. Level A relies only on publicly released, stable interfaces; level D covers non-recommended techniques such as modifications, direct write access to SAP tables or implicit enhancements, and carries the highest risk (ASUG).

Comparable concepts in other ERP systems

The principle is not unique to SAP. In Dynamics 365 Finance, Supply Chain and Commerce, for example, Microsoft has replaced customisation through "overlayering" with an extension model; Microsoft states that extensibility is the only customisation framework there and that overlayering is not supported (Microsoft Learn). Other vendors also provide extension concepts based on interfaces and platforms. Check the specific options for your system and release with the vendor.

Standalone services

Not every custom solution has to run on the ERP vendor's own platform. For some tasks – cross-system analytics, portals or AI functions, for instance – a standalone service that talks to the ERP through defined interfaces can be the better choice. It reduces dependence on the ERP's release cycle, but brings its own operating effort.

Make or buy: the decision criteria

Before you build, check whether a suitable product already exists. The table below helps frame the decision:

CriterionPoints towards buyingPoints towards building
DifferentiationThe process is standard for the industryThe process is a genuine competitive advantage
Market offeringEstablished products with references existNo product covers the requirement
Rate of changeRequirements are stableRequirements change often and quickly
OperationsNo in-house team for further developmentOwnership of operations and development is clear
LifespanNeeded long term and should stay close to standardA bridge, e.g. until harmonisation after a carve-out
DependencyVendor lock-in is acceptableData ownership and independence matter

Rarely do all criteria point the same way. What matters is that the decision is made deliberately and documented – and that you look at total cost over the solution's lifetime, not just development cost. If a platform change is on the horizon at the same time, the question belongs in that wider context; see RISE with SAP or alternatives.

Maintainability: custom solutions that don't become legacy

A custom solution is only good if someone can still understand and change it a few years from now. Pay attention to the following:

  • Clean interfaces: use only released, documented APIs, never direct table access.
  • Narrow scope: the solution does one thing, not "everything the standard can't".
  • Ownership: there is a named business owner and a named technical owner.
  • Upgrade readiness: every ERP update includes a test step for the custom solution.
  • Exit option: it is documented how the solution can be switched off if the standard covers the function later.

Document and test properly

Custom solutions rarely fail in their first version. They fail later, when changes are made without documentation or tests. A lean package works well:

  1. Business description: what problem does it solve, why is the standard not enough, and who made that call?
  2. Technical description: architecture, interfaces used, data flows, authorisations.
  3. Automated tests for the core flows, run with every release.
  4. Business test cases for acceptance and regression testing.
  5. Operations guide: monitoring, error handling, contacts.

At go-live in particular, custom solutions belong in the cutover plan and the mock cutovers. The article SAP cutover planning explains how to structure this.

Checklist: before you build a custom solution

  • Have you checked whether a process change would make the standard fit?
  • Have you checked whether a market product exists?
  • Does the ERP core remain unchanged (for SAP: clean core)?
  • Are only released interfaces used?
  • Are business and technical owners named?
  • Are documentation, automated tests and an operating concept in place?
  • Is an exit option described?

FAQ

Does a custom solution contradict the clean core principle?

No – not if it is built outside the core or through released interfaces. Clean core is about avoiding changes to the core, not about avoiding extensions.

What happens to existing custom code when moving to S/4HANA?

It should be inventoried and assessed before the move: what is still used, what does the new standard cover, and what needs to be rebuilt cleanly? Code nobody uses does not need to be migrated.

Does this apply to ERP systems other than SAP?

Yes. Terms and tools differ, but the principle – keep the core stable and extend through defined interfaces – applies across vendors.
